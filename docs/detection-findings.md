# Detecting Imbalance That Costs Latency

2026-10-07, phase 0: what offline data and a routing capture answer, before
anything runs online.
Numbers are regenerated by `scripts/detection_eval.py`, which writes
`detection.json`. The statistics live in `moe_reliability_results.detection`.

The pipeline under test screens on busiest-rank load, confirms against step
time, and attributes the step to a rank:

| Stage | Reads | Costs | Answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Screen | token-expert counts per layer and rank | always on | is load high? |
| 2. Confirm | engine step time by batch and prefill content | cheap, on demand | is it costing time? |
| 3. Attribute | a profiled window | expensive, rare | which rank, and why? |

## Answer so far

**The screen works, and it is cheap.** With a window of 8 requests counting 1.2-1.5%
of token-expert assignments (4 MoE layers, a tenth of the tokens), and the alarm
set at a 1% false-alarm rate on benign traffic, it flags **every** skew level
that costs latency on both models, and 80-100% of windows at the lowest costly
level. `figures/detection.png`.

**Window length buys more than counting more.** One request at 100% counted is
worse than 8 requests at 1.3%: a single prompt is itself skewed, so its benign
distribution is wide and the alarm has to sit high. On Mixtral, one request with
a tenth of the tokens forces an alarm at 2.28x and then detects 1% of windows at
1.39x, where 8 requests at an eighth the cost detect 80%.

**It localises.** With the bias confined to 6 of 26 MoE layers and placed on rank
2, the model-wide mean reads 1.40x - near the benign floor - while the worst
layer reads 2.58x. Flagging layers individually recovers **exactly the 6 biased
layers, precision and recall 1.0, and names rank 2**, at both skew levels, with
no layer flagged in the balanced arm. A detector that reports only a mean would
have missed this.

**A rotating skew is the case that decides the statistic.** Biasing a different
rank in each layer gives the same per-layer skew as a fixed target but leaves the
pooled statistic at 1.05-1.08x, i.e. benign. On Mixtral that skew costs full
price in latency and a pooled screen flags 5-10% of windows against the per-layer
screen's 100%. On DeepSeek the same skew costs nothing - so load alone cannot
tell a costly skew from a free one, which is what stage 2 is for (C3).

**Two outputs that come free with the counts and matter more than the headline
number:** which layers carry the skew, and whether one rank carries it in all of
them. In natural traffic the busiest rank leads only 34-35% of layers, against
the ~25% a purely rotating rank gives and the ~100% an injected rank bias gives.
There is no persistent hot rank in natural traffic, which is a second,
independent reason EPLB-style rank rebalancing has nothing to win here.

**One alarming window is not an alarm.** At a 1% per-window false-alarm rate the
screen fires during benign traffic every few hundred requests. Requiring two
consecutive alarming windows fixes it - 98-100% detection, 0-1% early firing,
one false alarm per 1200-1750 requests - and costs 16 requests of delay (C4).

**The overhead knob that matters is not the sampling fraction.** Counting less
saves little, because the screen is already the small term; what costs is how
often it triggers a confirmation, which was measured at +15-19% TPOT while it
runs. Requiring three consecutive alarming windows takes the amortised pipeline
cost from 6.1% to 0.2% *and* raises detection from 0.50 to 1.00, for 24-40
requests of delay (C5).

**What is still unmeasured:** the screen's own cost on the device, behaviour
under a skew that ramps or flickers rather than switching on (every capture is a
server whose bias is fixed for its lifetime), and stage 3's ability to attribute
(the balanced case already has a pace setter).

## A. Where a cost begins

From the controlled sweeps, interpolating to where TPOT clears the 2.2%
run-to-run drift floor (`detection.impact_threshold`):

| Model | Benign load | Impact threshold | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| DeepSeek-V2-Lite | 1.097x | **2.41x** | 1.31x |
| Mixtral 8x7B | 1.117x | **1.31x** | 0.20x |

DeepSeek's threshold falls in the gap between its swept levels (2.34x and
3.45x), so it is an interpolation across a jump, not a measured point; the
`rbias-graph-threshold-002` run fills that gap. Mixtral's is interpolated
between measured levels 0.25 and 0.5.

**These are not the same kind of number.** Mixtral's cost rises linearly from
the first offset, so its threshold is where the line crosses the noise floor.
DeepSeek is flat to 2.34x and then jumps, so its threshold is a step location.
A screen tuned on one model's threshold would be wrong on the other, which is
why calibration has to be per model and per deployment.

## B. What benign traffic does

Unbiased MMLU captures, 3000 DeepSeek and 1500 Mixtral prompts, prompt and
generated tokens pooled, busiest rank per MoE layer averaged over layers:

| | DeepSeek | Mixtral |
|---|---|---|
| Pooled over the whole capture | 1.097x | 1.117x |
| The pooled-layer form would read | 1.038x | 1.023x |
| Per-layer range | 1.024-1.192x | 1.041-1.299x |
| One topic (min-max over 19 / 11) | 1.097-1.134x | 1.101-1.155x |
| Busiest rank leads | 35% of layers | 34% of layers |

- **The per-layer statistic is the one to use.** Pooling layers first, as vLLM
  0.23's EPLB balancedness does, reads 1.04x and 1.02x - it lets a rank that is
  hot in one layer and cold in another cancel. For a rank hot in *every* layer,
  the case that costs latency, the pooled form reads 1.00x: perfectly balanced.
  Upstream main has since fixed this (`_compute_eplb_load_stats` reduces over
  ranks), but any monitoring built on the shipped 0.23 statistic is blind to
  exactly the condition it should catch.
- **Mixtral's single layers reach 1.299x**, essentially its 1.31x impact
  threshold, while its mean over layers stays at 1.117x. A per-layer alarm needs
  its own, higher threshold; the impact curve was measured against the mean.
- **No persistent hot rank.** 34-35% against the 25% of a uniformly rotating
  rank and the ~100% of an injected rank bias. Natural imbalance is a layer-local,
  rotating phenomenon, so it is not the kind of imbalance expert placement can
  fix, and a detector that reports only a model-wide mean describes it poorly.

## C. How cheap the screen can be, and what it then catches

`detection-capture` replays 1200 prompts over six workload families at each skew
level, with routing recorded per request, on both models. The alarm is calibrated
to a 1% false-alarm rate on that capture's own balanced arm; the question is then
what share of skewed windows it flags.

Detection rate by setting, at each level's realised load (\* = at or above that
model's impact threshold, so a miss matters):

| Setting | Counted | Alarm | DeepSeek 1.35x / 1.72x / 2.43x\* / 3.54x\* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 request, all | 100% | 1.32x | 0.95 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 |
| 1 request, 4 layers, 10% tokens | 1.5% | 1.75x | 0.05 / 0.35 / 1.00 / 1.00 |
| 8 requests, all | 100% | 1.15x | 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 |
| **8 requests, 4 layers, 10% tokens** | **1.5%** | 1.23x | **1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00** |

| Setting | Counted | Alarm | Mixtral 1.21x / 1.39x\* / 1.80x\* / 2.69x\* / 3.44x\* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 request, all | 100% | 1.42x | 0.10 / 0.62 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 |
| 1 request, 4 layers, 10% tokens | 1.2% | 2.40x | 0.02 / 0.02 / 0.04 / 0.69 / 1.00 |
| 8 requests, all | 100% | 1.19x | 0.93 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 |
| **8 requests, 4 layers, 10% tokens** | **1.2%** | 1.31x | 0.10 / **0.80** / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 |
| 64 requests, 4 layers, 10% tokens | 1.2% | 1.14x | 0.97 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 |

- **Mixtral is the binding case**, as its impact threshold (1.31x) sits closest
  to its benign floor (1.12x). Its lowest costly level, 1.39x, is caught in 80%
  of windows at 1.2% counted and 100% at full counting or a longer window.
- **DeepSeek is easy**: its threshold is 2.4x against a 1.10x floor, and every
  setting with a window catches everything.
- **The one failure is a single-request window with token sampling.** Its benign
  p99 reaches 1.41-1.54x, so a 1%-FPR alarm lands at 1.75x (DeepSeek) or 2.40x
  (Mixtral) and the screen goes blind to the loads that matter.
- **Longer windows trade delay for margin.** 64 requests at the same 1.2% cost
  pulls Mixtral's 1.21x detection from 0.10 to 0.97 - but 1.21x costs nothing, so
  that is sensitivity spent on a level the screen should arguably ignore.

## C2. Localising a skew that the mean hides

`detection-localisation` biases 6 of DeepSeek's 26 MoE layers, on rank 2:

| Level | Mean load | Worst layer | Layers flagged | Recall | Precision | Rank named |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1.08x | 1.15x | 0 | - | - | - |
| 1 | 1.40x | 2.58x | 6 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 2 |
| 2 | 1.67x | 3.69x | 6 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 2 |

The mean over layers dilutes the skew roughly fourfold, as designed: at level 1 it
reads 1.40x where the biased layers read 2.58x. Flagging per layer at a 1.3x
threshold recovers the biased set exactly and names the right rank, and flags
nothing in the balanced arm. **Nothing in the analysis assumes rank 0**, and a
skew that a model-wide average would hide is still found.

The caveat: this is a *persistent* per-layer skew, the same 6 layers for the
server's lifetime. Natural imbalance rotates between layers (34-35% rank
consistency), and whether localisation helps there is untested.

## C3. A rotating skew: where the pooled statistic fails completely

`rotate-calibration` biases rank *(layer mod 4)*, so every layer is skewed as
hard as a fixed rank target makes it but no rank is hot throughout. It is the
matched pair for the fixed-target sweeps, and the two statistics part company:

| Injected offset | Fixed target, per layer | Rotating, per layer | **Rotating, pooled** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1.14x | 1.14x | 1.02x |
| 0.5 | 1.66x | 1.71x | **1.05x** |
| 1 | 2.35x | 2.40x | **1.07x** |
| 2 | 3.45x | 3.53x | **1.08x** |

The per-layer loads match the fixed target to within 3%, so the skew really is
the same size. The pooled form - the one vLLM's EPLB balancedness computes -
reads **1.05-1.08x, which is indistinguishable from benign traffic at 1.10x**.

This is the sharp version of the warning in section B. There, pooling understated
natural traffic (1.04x against 1.10x) and a rank hot in every layer was the one
case the two agreed on. Here is the opposite extreme, constructed: a skew as
strong as anything we have injected, which a pooled monitor cannot see at all.

**It does cost, on Mixtral, and the pooled screen cannot see it.** The
`rotating-skew` sweeps ([router-bias-findings.md](router-bias-findings.md))
measured both models against the fixed-target sweeps at matched per-layer load:

| | Rotating costs | Fixed costs | Pooled screen detects | Per-layer screen detects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mixtral, 2.6x | **193.2 ms** | 193.9 ms | **0.05** | **1.00** |
| Mixtral, 1.75x | 176.4 ms | 176.6 ms | 0.10 | 1.00 |
| DeepSeek, 3.6x | 174.8 ms (flat) | 212.6 ms | 1.00 | 1.00 |

- **On Mixtral a rotating skew costs exactly what a fixed one costs** - the same
  millisecond at matched load - **and a pooled screen flags 5-10% of windows.**
  That is the case the per-layer statistic exists for: a real, full-price latency
  cost that the shipped EPLB statistic reports as a balanced server.
- **On DeepSeek a rotating skew costs nothing**, so the per-layer screen's 100%
  detection there is an alarm on something harmless. The pooled screen also fires
  (its balanced distribution is narrow enough that 1.08x clears a 1.04x alarm),
  but only by a hair.

**This is the clearest argument for stage 2.** Load alone cannot separate the
Mixtral case, which costs full price, from the DeepSeek case, which costs
nothing, because their *load* is the same. Only relating the flagged load to step
time distinguishes them, and the screen should be read as "look here", never as
"this is costing you".

## C4. How fast it fires, and how often it cries wolf

A per-window false-alarm rate of 1% sounds small and is not: over a run of 40
benign windows it is a one-in-three chance of firing before anything happens.
Requiring *k* consecutive alarming windows before raising one is the knob that
fixes it, and it costs delay. Measured by running a balanced stretch into a
skewed one, at the cheapest setting (8-request windows, 4 layers, a tenth of the
tokens), with the onset at each model's lowest costly level:

| Window | k | Detected | Fired early | Delay (requests) | Quiet for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 1 | 0.50 / 0.76 | **0.50 / 0.24** | 8 | 324 / 656 reqs |
| **8** | **2** | **0.98 / 1.00** | **0.01 / 0.00** | **16** | **1204 / 1752 reqs** |
| 8 | 3 | 1.00 / 1.00 | 0.00 / 0.00 | 24 / 40 | beyond horizon |
| 32 | 2 | 1.00 / 0.99 | 0.00 / 0.01 | 64 | 6016 / 8192 reqs |
| 128 | 2 | 0.95 / 0.96 | 0.05 / 0.04 | 256 | 16896 / 31744 reqs |

(DeepSeek / Mixtral. "Quiet for" is the median number of requests benign traffic
runs before a false alarm.)

- **A single alarming window is not an alarm.** At k = 1 the screen fires during
  the balanced stretch in 24-75% of runs, and benign traffic trips it every
  324-656 requests. Any deployment would turn it off within a day.
- **Two consecutive windows is the setting to use.** Detection goes to 98-100%,
  early firing to 0-1%, and the wait between false alarms to 1200-1750 requests,
  for 16 requests of delay - two windows, which is the minimum k = 2 can cost.
- **Longer windows buy quiet and pay for it in delay, linearly.** 128-request
  windows at k = 2 run 17-32k requests between false alarms but take 256
  requests to notice. Since the skew being detected is a standing condition
  rather than a transient, that trade is available but rarely worth it.
- **The delay distribution is tight** (p50 = p90 at most settings): once the skew
  starts, the next window sees it. The variance is in whether the *alarm* was
  already tripped, not in how long detection takes.

This is a steady-state result. The onset is instantaneous and the skew then
persists, because every capture is a server whose bias is fixed for its lifetime;
a skew that ramps or flickers is not represented.

## C5. Trading accuracy against overhead

Three knobs change the cost, and they do not act on the same term:

| Knob | Changes | Effect on accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Layers and tokens counted | the always-on screen | widens the benign spread, so the alarm rises |
| Window length | the always-on screen, slightly | longer is tighter, and slower to react |
| **Consecutive windows (k)** | **how often confirmation fires** | more is quieter, and slower to react |

**The counting is not the expensive part.** The screen at its cheapest setting
touches 1.2-1.5% of token-expert assignments. What costs is what the screen
*triggers*: a profiled confirmation was measured at **+15-19% TPOT while it runs**
(`rbias-rotating-profiled`, which serves each point unprofiled and profiled).
Paying that every time the screen cries wolf dominates everything else.

Amortised pipeline cost, taking a confirmation as +19% for 100 requests and the
screen itself as 0.2% (a placeholder - the screen's device cost is still
unmeasured):

| | w | k | Detect | Delay | Benign quiet for | **Cost, no stage-2 filter** | Cost, perfect filter |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DeepSeek | 8 | 1 | 0.50 | 8 | 324 reqs | **6.06%** | 0.20% |
| | 8 | 2 | 0.98 | 16 | 1204 reqs | 1.78% | 0.20% |
| | **8** | **3** | **1.00** | **24** | **never fired** | **0.20%** | 0.20% |
| | 128 | 2 | 0.95 | 256 | 16896 reqs | 0.31% | 0.20% |
| Mixtral | 8 | 1 | 0.76 | 8 | 656 reqs | **3.10%** | 0.20% |
| | 8 | 2 | 1.00 | 16 | 1752 reqs | 1.28% | 0.20% |
| | **8** | **3** | **1.00** | **40** | **never fired** | **0.20%** | 0.20% |
| | 128 | 2 | 0.96 | 256 | 31744 reqs | 0.26% | 0.20% |

- **k is the lever, not the sampling fraction.** Going from k = 1 to k = 3 at an
  8-request window takes the amortised cost from 6.1% to 0.2% on DeepSeek and
  3.1% to 0.2% on Mixtral, *and* raises detection from 0.50 to 1.00. It costs 16
  to 32 requests of extra delay. Nothing else on this table buys that much.
- **Cutting the counting further would be the wrong economy.** At 1.2-1.5% of
  assignments the screen is already the small term; halving it while leaving
  k = 1 in place saves 0.1% and leaves 6%.
- **The two columns bracket what stage 2 is worth.** If the cheap confirmation
  rejects every false alarm before the profiler runs, the pipeline costs only the
  screen; if it rejects none, the right-hand column applies. How well it actually
  filters is unmeasured - it is the same question as whether stage 2 can separate
  a costly skew from a free one (C3).
- **k = 3 with an 8-request window is the setting to recommend**: full detection,
  24-40 requests of delay, no false alarm within the simulated horizon, and no
  confirmation cost to amortise.

The unmeasured number remains the screen's own device cost. Everything above
treats it as 0.2%; `detection.monitoring_overhead` prices it from one
measurement - the per-assignment cost of a `scatter_add_` into a resident buffer
- which has to be taken on the device.

## D. Stage 2: step time at matched batch size

Slope of median step wall against busiest-rank load, within a batch bin, from
the eager profiled runs (offsets 0 / 1 / 2):

| Step kind | DeepSeek | Mixtral |
|---|---|---|
| Decode, batch 1-200 | +36.9 ms (+24%) | +3.3 ms (+2.5%) |
| Decode, batch 350-480 | +39.7 ms (+24%) | -0.9 ms (-0.6%) |
| Decode, batch 480+ | +1.4 ms (+0.9%) | -0.2 ms (-0.1%) |
| Prefill-carrying, batch 480+ | +5.6 ms (+2.6%) | +154.4 ms (+70%) |

**Stage 2 works, but only if it conditions on the kind of step.** The two models
put the cost in different places - DeepSeek in below-full-batch decode steps,
Mixtral in prefill-carrying ones - and both read ~0 in saturated decode steps,
which are the majority. A stage-2 test that averaged over a window would find
nothing on either model. This is the same effect that made window-averaged trace
figures disagree with TPOT by 1.5-3x.

These slopes include the measurement plugin's own cost, since the profiled runs
predate `bias_plugin_at_zero`. They are usable as a shape, not as a calibration.

## E. Stage 3: which rank paces the step

From `collective_wait` in the trace summaries, the rank that arrives last at the
collective:

| Level | DeepSeek | Mixtral |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (balanced) | rank 3, 83% of calls | rank 0, 79% |
| 1 | rank 0, 52% | rank 0, 72% |
| 2 | rank 0, 67% | rank 0, 86% |

**Stage 3 identifies the biased rank under skew, but the balanced case already
has a pace setter**, and on Mixtral it happens to be rank 0 - the same rank the
bias targets. With one profiled point per level there is no way to tell a real
attribution from that coincidence. Stage 3 needs a per-run balanced baseline
before it can attribute anything, which matches the earlier finding that one
arbitrary rank paces each server instance and it differs every run.

## F. What confirmation costs

**Unmeasured.** `separate_profiling_run` serves each point twice, unprofiled for
the timings and profiled for the traces, and the profiled pass's timings were
discarded. That is now kept (`profiled_request_summary`), so the next profiled
run measures the profiler's own cost within a point at no extra NPU time.

The screen's cost is not measured either. `detection.monitoring_overhead` prices
a device-side counter from one number - the per-assignment cost of a
`scatter_add_` into a resident buffer - which has to be measured on the device.
What is already known is the shape of the risk: the router-bias plugin's
per-call tensor add costs 10.0 ms per token on eager DeepSeek, where decode is
host-bound, and nothing on Mixtral. A monitor doing per-call host work would
disturb exactly what it measures.

## Workload families

Section B's benign numbers are MMLU only, which is one dataset and 19 topics.
Sections C and C2 use six families - MMLU, GSM8K, MBPP, MMMLU (German), Dolly and
UltraChat - interleaved by a `mixed:` spec so a window of consecutive prompts
holds all of them, 200 prompts each. That is the benign distribution the alarms
there are calibrated on, and it is the harder test: a window spanning six
corpora varies more than one spanning MMLU topics.

The mix also shifts realised load. At the same injected offset the six-family
capture reads 1.35x where the MMLU-only calibration reads 1.30x (DeepSeek,
offset 0.25) and 1.72x against 1.66x at offset 0.5. **Which traffic a deployment
serves moves its rank load by a few percent**, so a threshold calibrated on one
corpus does not transfer exactly to another.

## Open

1. **Measure the screen's per-assignment cost on the device.** The accuracy side
   of the frontier is now measured; the cost side is still a placeholder. A
   `scatter_add_` into a resident counter inside the expert-selection wrapper is
   the implementation to time, against the same unprofiled twin the profiler
   overhead will use.
2. **A skew that ramps or flickers.** C4 measures an instantaneous onset into a
   standing skew, which is the easy case. The plugin would have to read its bias
   from a tensor updated in place - which also keeps graph mode working, since
   the vector is allocated once and never replaced - to script a ramp.
3. **Stage 2 on a rotating skew.** C3 shows load alone cannot separate Mixtral's
   costly rotating skew from DeepSeek's free one. The step tables that would test
   whether stage 2 can are not captured for the rotating sweeps, which were not
   profiled.
4. **A balanced baseline per profiled run**, so stage 3 can attribute rather
   than coincide.
5. **Time-varying skew.** Every measurement here is a server whose bias is fixed
   for its lifetime, so detection *delay* is unmeasured. The plugin would have
   to read its bias from a tensor updated in place, which also keeps graph mode
   working.
